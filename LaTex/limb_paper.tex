\begin{abstract}
We analyze 29 low-phase-angle \textit{Cassini} Visual and Infrared Mapping Spectrometer (VIMS) full-disk observations acquired from 2004 to 2017. We fit north- and south-hemisphere radial brightness profiles with a quadratic limb-darkening law and use $u_1+u_2$ as the signed measure of limb darkening or brightening. The north-minus-south median changes sign between northern winter and northern spring in both VIMS channels. For the visible channel the medians are $0.168$ and $-0.110$, and for the infrared channel they are $0.035$ and $-0.376$, respectively. Whole-observation bootstrap intervals exclude zero for both visible phases and for infrared spring, but not for infrared winter. The sign change remains under tested emission-angle and fit-quality rules. These results show a seasonal change in Titan's hemispheric limb profiles while leaving detailed atmospheric retrieval to future work.

\end{abstract}
\keywords{Titan, Upper atmosphere, Seasonal phenomena}


\section{INTRODUCTION}

Titan's thick nitrogen atmosphere has proven to be strongly dynamic, responding to seasonal insolation trends in a complex manner.  In addition to clouds \citep{Rodriguez2009Global, Schaller_etal09, Griffith2009Characterization, Brown2010Clouds, VIMSclouds, Rodriguez2011Clouds, Turtle2018Titan} and dust storms \citep{rodriguez2018observational} in the lower atmosphere, Titan's upper atmospheric circulation varies seasonally as evidenced by variations in its haze aerosols \citep{rannou2002wind, Tomasko2005Titan, seignovert2021haze}.

From the Voyager 1 encounter in 1980, \citet{smith1981encounter} showed a north-south hemispheric dichotomy in the observed haze brightness.  Except for distant Voyager 2 imaging \citep{smith1982new}, Titan remained unresolved until the Hubble Space Telescope (HST) based observations were again able to pick up on haze variations \citep{caldwell1992titan, lorenz1997titan, lorenz1999seasonal, lorenz2001titan, young2002three, lorenz2004seasonal, Ádámkovics2006Titan, karkoschka2016seasonal, karkoschka2022titan}.  With adaptive optics, Titan's hemispheric dichotomy can be measured from ground-based observatories as well \citep{gibbard2004titan, adámkovics2017titan, nichols2021tracking}.

The arrival of Cassini into the Saturn system in 2004 provided a new opportunity to monitor Titan repeatedly and from close range.  The Imaging Science Subsystem (ISS) instrument, a fine-resolution visible-light camera, observed extensive variations of the haze with latitude and time \citep{porco2005titan, dekok2010titan,snell2024titan}.  Cassini's Visual and Infrared Mapping Spectrometer (VIMS), an imaging spectrometer, mapped haze distributions across a factor of 10 in wavelength from $0.4-5.2~\mathrm{\mu m}$.  VIMS also identified seasonal variations \citep{seignovert2021haze,vashist2023titan} along with diagnostic spatial features \citep{kutsop2022titan}.

The different haze appearance, including the hemispheric dichotomy, results from a non-obvious interplay between haze concentration, haze single-scattering albedo, atmospheric absorption, and the vertical structure of the variation of those parameters.  For instance, while the north-south dichotomy remains, the brightness ratio between the hemispheres can be different when viewed at different wavelengths --- in the visible versus in the near-infrared for instance \citep{vashist2023titan}.  While the evidence of the vertical variation in haze and atmosphere may be represented in Titan spectra \citep{rannou2010titan}, retrievals of atmospheric profiles from spectra alone remain non-unique.

Cassini employed a few different techniques that isolate the haze's vertical structure.  In particular, ISS was able to monitor conditions of the ``detached haze" --- a thin, discrete high-altitude layer of haze enhancement first seen by Voyager 1 \citep{smith1981encounter}.  \citet{lavvas2009titan} and \citet{seignovert2017titan}  constrained properties of the aerosol particles within a particular detached haze layer.  The haze dropped sharply in altitude from $\sim500$~km down to $\sim370$~km at the time of Titan's northern spring equinox in 2008 \citep{west2011evolution}.  The detached haze later disappeared in 2012 and reappeared later in 2016 \cite{west2018seasonal}.

The other technique that reveals vertical structure uses occultations.  When a star, or the Sun, passes behind the limb of Titan as viewed from Cassini, the spectral lightcurve of the occultation depends on vertical atmospheric variations.  VIMS observed a set of 14 relevant occultations \citep{sotin2016characteristics, robinson2014titan}, from which atmospheric absorptions \citep{bellucci2009titan} and haze abundance could be ascertained at those particular times and places \citep{kim2013spectral, maltagliati2015titan, rannnou2022analysis}.  Observations of stellar occultations in ultraviolet wavelengths \citep{koskinen2011mesosphere} showed intricate structure unable to be adequately fit by simple haze parameterizations \citep{tribbett2021titan}.

We suggest that it may be possible to use limb darkening across Titan's spatially resolved disk at wavelengths with differing degrees of gaseous absorption to probe haze properties as a function of altitude.  In this paper, we report observed limb darkening or limb brightening parameters for Titan's haze throughout the Cassini mission, leaving detailed analysis and modeling for future efforts.  We discuss our observations from the VIMS instrument in Section \ref{section:observations}, and the methods that we use to analyze those observations as well as a simple two-layer model for their interpretation in Section \ref{section:methods}.  We then discuss the results of the analysis in Section \ref{section:results} before concluding in Section \ref{section:conclusion}.

The VIMS instruments have been well characterized \cite{brown2004cassini}, with the VIMS-V (Visual) channel of the using a two-dimensional array detector covering a spectral range from 0.30 to 1.05 µm with 96 distinct bands and the VIMS-IR (Infrared) channel using a one-dimensional detector covering a broader spectral range of 0.3 to 5.1 µm with 256 wavelength bands.
\subsection{Cube Selection}

We selected flybys using the following criteria: (1) simultaneous observations from both the VIMS-V and VIMS-IR instruments; (2) a low phase angle (<30\textdegree{} preferred); (3) full-disk coverage with high limb visibility; (4) sufficient spatial resolution (200km/pixel); (5) sub-spacecraft longitude between 20\textdegree{}S and 20\textdegree{}N; and (6) sufficient time cadence so as to obtain a comprehensive distribution of measurements during the \textit{Cassini} mission. It is important to note that during the latter half of the \textit{Cassini} mission, the spacecraft was in a polar orbit, which limited the number of flybys that met the above criteria, leading to the selection of non-targeted flybys, expansion of criteria, and sparser temporal coverage.

\section{Methods}\label{section:methods}
\subsection{SRTC++} 
We use 1,889 SRTC++ image cases to test how a two-layer atmosphere can change the fitted limb profile. The grid varies haze abundance, single-scattering albedo, and gas contribution in lower and upper layers. We treat the SRTC++ renderer as the source of the synthetic images and do not infer its internal physical assumptions from the image set. We rebuild $u_1+u_2$ and the image-quality flag from every TIFF. All 1,889 quality flags match the saved run, and the median absolute coefficient drift is $4.7\times10^{-10}$. As a check on the mapping from inputs to limb behavior, we compare a random forest, histogram gradient boosting, and a scaled radial-basis support-vector regressor using one fixed 80/20 split. The best held-out result is from the random forest, with $R^2=0.990$. Its largest feature importance is the upper-layer single-scattering albedo. These model scores describe interpolation within this grid and do not validate the atmospheric assumptions of SRTC++.


\subsection{Image Processing}
\label{sec: image processing}

We remove VIMS-V column striping by subtracting each column's mean sky signal and restoring the mean background level. We do not destripe VIMS-IR or SRTC++ images. A fixed one-based band list excludes known surface windows and the long-wavelength range where fit coverage and quality decline. Eight of 352 bands fail our separate band-health check; all lie between 3.84 and 4.78~$\mathrm{\mu m}$ and are already outside the main analysis range.


We estimate the disk center from the lowest emission-angle pixels and find image north from the latitude field. We draw radial lines on a grid enlarged by a factor of five, then map the selected pixels back to the native cube. For each band we select transects 30\textdegree{} north and south of the projected equator on the illuminated side of the disk. We sort each profile by emission angle and retain points with $25\textdegree{} < e \leq 89\textdegree{}$. This is the rule used by the original production run; the old folder name suggesting a north--south-boundary mask did not reflect the active path. The result is two profiles for each of 352 bands in each cube, or 20,416 profiles in total. The current fixed software stack defines the accepted line raster. We retain a row-level comparison with the earlier raster as an audit record.



\subsection{Quadratic Limb Darkening Law Regression}
To determine the magnitude of limb darkening and brightening within each transect, we fit the data using the known stellar Quadratic Limb Darkening Law (QLDL) from \cite{kopal1950limb, brown2001hubble}. 

\begin{equation}
    \frac{I(\mu)}{I(1)} = \left[1 - u_{1}(1 - \mu) - u_{2}(1 - \mu)^2\right]
\end{equation}

Where $\mu$ is the cosine of the angle between a line normal to the stellar surface and the line of sight of the observer leading to a scale from 1 to 0, one representing the center of the disk and 0 being the limb. $I(1)$ represents the intensity at the center of the disk. $u_{1}$ and $u_{2}$ are limb darkening coefficients. The limb darkening coefficients alone are not well constrained. So to measure the magnitude of limb darkening/brightening, we use $\left.\frac{1}{I(1)}\frac{dI}{d\mu}\right|_{\mu=0.5} = u_{1} + u_{2}$.

We map emission angle to $\mu$, interpolate each profile to 200 points with a shape-preserving cubic interpolator, and test the interpolated profile, a Gaussian-smoothed profile, and a moving-average profile. We fit $I(1)$, $u_1$, and $u_2$ by nonlinear least squares, constrain only $I(1)$ to be non-negative, and retain the candidate with the highest $R^2$ against the sampled profile. The covariance matrix gives the variance of $u_1+u_2$ as $\mathrm{var}(u_1)+\mathrm{var}(u_2)+2\,\mathrm{cov}(u_1,u_2)$. We add north and south variances when reporting the error of their difference.

Visually, the limb darkening is prevalent at lower, visible, wavelengths, while the limb brightening is more prominent at higher, near-infrared, wavelengths, though the wavelength where darkening and brightening transition seemingly evolves. The differences in limb darkening and brightening are logical when factoring the low albedo of haze at visible wavelengths and the increased haze-ratio at higher altitudes. Limb observations also reinforce existing knowledge of the north-south asymmetry's visual shift, with a strong hemispheric dichotomy seen in most bands.


\subsection{Quality, Seasonal, and Sensitivity Analysis}
Fits fail the main quality gate if fitting fails, fewer than six profile points remain, a required value is not finite, or the covariance matrix is invalid. Fits with negative $R^2$ remain in the data as review cases but do not enter the main analysis. We apply no hard coefficient bound. Of 20,416 fits, 19,791 are eligible, 397 require review, and 228 fail. We calculate north-minus-south values only for bands where both fits are eligible.

We reduce each cube and VIMS channel to the median across allowed bands before comparing seasons, so bands do not act as independent observations. Fixed-seed bootstrap intervals resample whole cubes 5,000 times. Northern winter contains 12 cubes, northern spring 16, and northern summer one. We do not report an interval for the one-cube summer group. Our sensitivity grid repeats the full fit and analysis for emission cutoffs of 20\textdegree{}, 25\textdegree{}, and 30\textdegree{}, minimum $R^2$ values of 0, 0.25, and 0.5, and coefficient bounds of none and 10.
\section{Results}\label{section:results}

The main run yields 4,067 paired band measurements. In northern winter, the median north-minus-south $u_1+u_2$ difference is $0.168$ for VIMS-V (95\% bootstrap interval $0.133$ to $0.243$) and $0.035$ for VIMS-IR ($-0.141$ to $0.392$). In northern spring, the values are $-0.110$ for VIMS-V ($-0.331$ to $-0.058$) and $-0.376$ for VIMS-IR ($-0.509$ to $-0.265$). Northern summer has only one observation and remains descriptive.

The sign change is stable across all 18 sensitivity settings. Winter medians range from $0.167$ to $0.192$ in VIMS-V and $0.030$ to $0.072$ in VIMS-IR. Spring medians range from $-0.122$ to $-0.110$ in VIMS-V and $-0.377$ to $-0.373$ in VIMS-IR. Depending on the rule, 15,380 to 19,893 fits and 3,712 to 4,070 north--south pairs remain. Two series have more than one darkening-to-brightening crossing in the least strict settings. We show all crossings but exclude multi-crossing series from scalar transition summaries.

\section{Conclusion}\label{section:conclusion}

Titan's north--south limb-profile difference changes sign from northern winter to northern spring in both VIMS channels. This result survives the tested profile and fit-quality choices. The visible winter and spring intervals and the infrared spring interval exclude zero; the infrared winter interval does not. The single northern-summer cube cannot support a phase-level claim. Fit covariance, whole-observation resampling, band-health checks, and the full sensitivity grid now make the limits of these results explicit. A later physical retrieval should test whether changes in upper-layer haze scattering can reproduce the observed time and wavelength pattern.

% After we generate a final set of images, we adapt the methods from \cite{Roman2009} to determine the latitude of the NSA boundary. We shift the images by 6\textdegree{}N and 6\textdegree{}S and then subtract to create a brightness contrast of each image, which highlights the presence of an asymmetry \citep{Roman2009}. These maps were then sequentially analyzed along each longitude using a sixth-order polynomial fit to smooth out signal variations. We found the locations of critical points using the derivative function of the fit; extraneous values, such as imaginary solutions and numbers outside of the latitude range, were removed to leave only the latitude location of the NSA at each longitude column. Our algorithm then finds the latitude value of the NSA transition for all the columns within each projection and then averages them to determine the location of the NSA for each band in the flyby. Since each column has a varied value for the NSA, we applied a moving average to the data to flatten irregularities in column brightness data. Using the latitude value found in each image, we apply a simple average of the brightness 30\textdegree{}N and 30\textdegree{}S of the NSA transition to determine the North/South Flux Ratio. We derive I/F values using average visible latitude, where each latitude is an average of all visible longitudinal brightness values.
